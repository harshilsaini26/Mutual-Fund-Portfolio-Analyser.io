    How mutual fund gains are taxed

    The ideas behind the tax on fund gains and payouts. Rates change with each Budget, so none are printed here.

    This explains how things work. It is not advice about what to buy, sell or hold.

    On this page

    1. What is taxed
    2. Short term and long term
    3. Equity funds and the rest
    4. Where the current rules are

    What is taxed

    Selling units for more than they cost produces a capital gain, and the gain is what is taxed, not the whole amount received. Payouts under the IDCW option are different: they are taxed as income of the investor in the year they are paid, as described under Growth and IDCW options.

    Short term and long term

    A gain is short-term or long-term depending on how long the units were held before being sold. The holding period that makes a gain long-term is not the same for every kind of fund, and long-term gains are generally treated more lightly than short-term ones. For many debt funds bought since April 2023, every gain counts as short-term, however long the units are held.

    Equity funds and the rest

    Funds that invest mainly in shares of Indian companies are taxed as equity-oriented funds; debt funds and many other funds are taxed differently, and recent Finance Acts have changed how debt fund gains are treated. Equity Linked Savings Schemes carry a deduction under the old tax regime, with a lock-in.

    Where the current rules are

    Tax rates and holding periods are set by Parliament in each year's Finance Act and change from Budget to Budget, so this site prints none of them. AMFI keeps a page on the treatment, dated by financial year, linked below; check the year it covers. A fund's scheme information document describes it too.
