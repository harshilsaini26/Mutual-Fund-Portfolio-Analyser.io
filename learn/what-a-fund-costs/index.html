    What a fund costs

    The expense ratio, how it is taken, how a yearly cost adds up, and the other charges a fund can carry.

    This explains how things work. It is not advice about what to buy, sell or hold.

    The expense ratio

    A fund's running costs (managing the money, administration, registrar and custodian fees, distribution and the like) are added up and stated as a yearly percentage of its assets: the total Expense ratio (TER). SEBI sets the maximum, in regulation 52 of its mutual fund rules, and within that total a fund may spend on any allowed kind of cost.

    Taken every day, not billed

    No bill arrives. A sliver of the yearly cost is taken from the fund's assets every day, so a 1% yearly ratio means about a 365th of 1% each day. The NAV (net asset value) published each evening is already net of it, which is why the cost never appears as a separate line.

    How a yearly cost adds up

    A cost is taken whatever the year brings, and what is taken no longer grows. Suppose ₹1,00,000 earns 10% a year before costs for twenty years. With a 1% yearly cost it grows at about 9% and ends near ₹5.60 lakh; with a 2% cost it grows at about 8% and ends near ₹4.66 lakh. The arithmetic is only an illustration of compounding, not a forecast of any fund.

    Other charges

    SEBI abolished entry loads, so no load is charged on buying, though a distributor may deduct a small transaction charge from a large purchase, as the scheme's documents state. A scheme may charge an exit load: a percentage of the NAV deducted from the proceeds when units are redeemed within a period it states, such as 1% within 90 days. The scheme information document sets out its loads.

    On this site

    Each fund page shows the expense ratio from AMFI's monthly list, and the Peers section sets it beside those of the category. The guide to Direct and Regular plans explains why the same scheme has two ratios.
